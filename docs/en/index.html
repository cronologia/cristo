Cristo — Chronology

Chronology of Jesus of Nazareth as the sources date him, of the objects venerated as his relics, and of where those objects are kept today

An open, source-referenced chronology of Jesus of Nazareth — the dates the Gospels, the ancient historians and modern scholars give for his life; the first places built to remember it — and of the objects venerated as relics of his life and Passion: when each first appears in the record, what was done to and with it, what laboratories have found, and which church, museum or community keeps it today.

About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). The life of Jesus is dated as the sources date it: the Gospels are cited as the texts that narrate it, the ancient historians and modern scholars for the dates they establish, and where they disagree — the year of the birth, the year and day of the crucifixion — the disagreement is recorded rather than resolved. Miracles and the Resurrection are recorded as what the Gospels narrate and the Church believes; the site neither asserts nor denies them. The relics are recorded object by object: each entry states one authority's act about one named object and cites the document. Expositions, feasts, indulgences, jubilees and papal visits are acts of cult, not rulings on authenticity, and no Church act declaring any of these objects authentic was located. Laboratory results belong to their laboratories; published critiques are recorded as critiques. Years before the common era are written BCE. Dates resting on a single source, or on a source that could not be read in full, are flagged with a ? mark. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

Shroud of Turin
Turin Cathedral, Italy, sealed and normally not visible. Owned by the Holy See since 1983; kept by the Archbishop of Turin as Pontifical Custodian. Radiocarbon-dated in 1988 to 1260–1390, a result contested by later critiques; the custodian says the tradition "cannot yet be said to be definitively proven".[38] [39] [48]
Sudarium of Oviedo
Cámara Santa of Oviedo Cathedral, Spain. First attested in 1075. Radiocarbon results are reported for 1990–1992 and 2007, but no primary publication of them was located.[57] [58]
Veil of Veronica and the Volto Santo
St Peter's Basilica, Vatican City, exposed each fifth Sunday of Lent; and the Capuchin shrine of Manoppello, Italy, which claims the original Veronica for itself. The two claims conflict.[60] [62]
Holy Tunic
Trier Cathedral, Germany (first attested 1196; not visible, its authenticity "cannot be answered with certainty", per the cathedral); and the Basilica of Saint-Denys, Argenteuil, France (first attested 1156; radiocarbon-dated to 530–650, per the parish, which last exposed it in April–May 2025).[65] [66] [67]
Holy Blood
Basilica of the Holy Blood, Bruges, Belgium, venerated daily; the procession is on UNESCO's intangible-heritage list. The basilica records that historians strongly doubt the traditional arrival in 1150.[69] [70]
True Cross
Fragments at Santa Croce in Gerusalemme (Rome; relic chapel closed from April 2026), St Peter's, Notre-Dame de Paris, the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem and the Mother See of Etchmiadzin, among many others. First attested c. 348–350; no scientific dating of the wood was located.[82] [77] [97] [71]
Titulus Crucis
Santa Croce in Gerusalemme, Rome. Found in 1492; radiocarbon-dated in 2002 to AD 980–1146.[82] [83]
Holy Nails
Santa Croce (Rome), Notre-Dame de Paris, the Iron Crown of Monza and the pin of Vienna's Holy Lance, among some thirty venerated nails; the Catholic Encyclopedia holds that "very little reliance" can be placed on most.[82] [86] [85]
Crown of Thorns
Notre-Dame de Paris, since its return on 13 December 2024; shown every Friday and venerated on the first Friday of the month. In Paris since 1239.[87]
Holy Lances
At least four objects: St Peter's, Vatican City (received 1492); the Imperial Treasury, Vienna (an 8th-century lance, per its museum); Etchmiadzin, Armenia; and Kraków. Which, if any, is the lance "found" at Antioch in 1098 is disputed.[93] [94] [97] [92]
Scala Sancta
Pontifical Sanctuary of the Scala Santa, Rome, kept by the Passionists; venerated as the stairs of Pilate's palace by a tradition the sanctuary reports as tradition.[98]
Columns of the Flagellation
Two rival claims: a black-and-white column at Santa Prassede, Rome, and a red porphyry fragment in the Franciscan chapel of the Holy Sepulchre, Jerusalem. Different stones. The Custody's own pages date the Jerusalem fragment's arrival at the Holy Sepulchre differently: 1553 and the 14th century.[102] [77] [103]
Holy Chalice
Valencia Cathedral, Spain: an agate cup the cathedral dates to the 1st century BC, with medieval mounts, venerated by tradition as the cup of the Last Supper. Since 2015 the Apostolic Penitentiary has made it a perpetual jubilee site — an act of cult. Genoa's Sacro Catino, once called the Grail, was shown in 1806 to be glass.[104] [105] [106]
What the Church has decided about authenticity
No Church act declaring any of these objects authentic was located. John Paul II said in 1998 that the Church "has no specific competence to pronounce" on the Shroud; the Holy See's 2017 instruction on relics concerns relics of the Blessed and the Saints. Expositions, feasts, indulgences and papal visits are acts of cult, recorded as such.[46] [109]

Chronology

Key events in chronological order. A ? flag marks dates not yet verified against a primary source.

YearDatePlaceEvent
10–1 BCE
6 BCE ? Bethlehem The birth of Jesus, as Matthew dates it: "in the days of King Herod" — Matthew narrates that Jesus "was born in Bethlehem of Judea, in the days of King Herod", and that the family fled to Egypt and returned only "when Herod had died". Scholars who follow Matthew's anchor place the birth a few years before 4 BCE.[3] [17] [18]A range, not a date. Maas (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) derives 7–5 BCE from Herod's death and Matthew 2:16; the encyclopedia's "General Chronology" reports that "many" place the birth in 4 BCE. Both put it before Herod's death, which Luke's census (6 CE) does not.
4 BCE Judea Death of Herod the Great — Josephus places Herod's death shortly after an eclipse of the moon; the Catholic bishops' Bible notes that he "reigned from 37 to 4 B.C." Matthew's birth narrative is set in his reign, so the birth of Jesus falls before 4 BCE by Matthew's account.[11] [20] [3]The majority date, from Josephus: the death follows a lunar eclipse that Whiston and the Catholic Encyclopedia identify as that of 12–13 March 4 BCE. A minority argues for 1 BCE (Filmer, 1966; Steinmann, 2009); those studies were not read for this dataset.
0s
6 Bethlehem Luke's census "when Quirinius was governor of Syria" — Luke narrates that Joseph and Mary went to Bethlehem to be enrolled in "the first enrollment, when Quirinius was governor of Syria". Quirinius became legate of Syria in 6–7 CE, about a decade after Herod's death. The Catholic bishops' own Bible notes that there are "notorious historical problems" with this dating and that "the various attempts to resolve the difficulties have proved unsuccessful".[1] [11]The census date is well attested (6–7 CE); that Jesus was born at it is Luke's narrative, which cannot be reconciled with Matthew's Herod-era birth.
10s
14 Rome, Italy Tiberius succeeds Augustus — Tiberius becomes emperor. Luke's "fifteenth year of Tiberius", which dates the start of John the Baptist's preaching, is counted from his reign.[2] [11]19 August 14, per Whiston's note; the year is secure.
18 ? Jerusalem Caiaphas becomes high priest — Joseph Caiaphas is appointed high priest by the Roman prefect Valerius Gratus; his father-in-law Annas had held the office in 6–15. Both appear in the Gospels' account of the Passion.[22] [2]"About A.D. 18" (Catholic Encyclopedia); the bishops' Bible note gives Caiaphas's high priesthood as 18–36.
20s
26 Caesarea Maritima, Israel Pontius Pilate becomes prefect of Judea — Pilate succeeds Valerius Gratus; the bishops' Bible note calls him "prefect of Judea from A.D. 26 to 36". An inscription found at Caesarea in 1961 gives his title as prefect; Tacitus and later writers call him procurator.[2] [21] [11]
28 ? Jordan River "The fifteenth year of Tiberius": John the Baptist begins to preach — Luke dates the start of John's preaching by a full set of anchors: the fifteenth year of Tiberius, when Pilate governed Judea and Herod Antipas Galilee, "during the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas".[2] [17]The bishops' Bible note puts the fifteenth year of Tiberius "between A.D. 27 and 29"; counted from an earlier co-regency it falls c. 26; Maas gives 25/26 or 29.
28 ? Jordan River The baptism of Jesus and the start of his ministry — The Gospels narrate that Jesus was baptized by John and began to preach; Luke says he was "about thirty years of age". The Synoptic Gospels can be read as describing a single year of ministry; John mentions three Passovers.[2] [17] [29]A range, c. 27–29 on Luke's synchronism; Maas, reckoning from a 7–5 BCE birth, puts the public life earlier.
30s
30 ? Jerusalem The crucifixion of Jesus under Pontius Pilate — The Gospels narrate that Jesus was condemned by Pilate and crucified at "the Place of the Skull, in Hebrew, Golgotha". Tacitus writes that "Christus … had undergone the death penalty in the reign of Tiberius, by sentence of the procurator Pontius Pilatus" — the Roman historian's independent witness to the execution.[10] [8] [12] [29] [30] [17]Two dates remain in play: Friday 7 April 30 and Friday 3 April 33 (Julian). Humphreys and Waddington favour 33 but write that "the date 7 April AD 30 is also strongly supported" and that the question cannot be decided conclusively; a patristic tradition cited by Maas gives 29. The Synoptic Gospels place the death on the day after the Passover meal, John on the day of preparation; Mark says the crucifixion was at the third hour, John that the sentence came "about noon".
30 ? Jerusalem The empty tomb and the Resurrection, as proclaimed — The Gospels narrate that women found the tomb open "very early … on the first day of the week"; Paul hands on an earlier formula, that Christ "was raised on the third day" and "appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve". Christians profess the Resurrection; historians treat the proclamation as the earliest disciples' claim. This site records both, and adjudicates neither.[9] [6]"The first day of the week" after the crucifixion — so 9 April 30 or 5 April 33, depending on the crucifixion year.
30 ? Jerusalem The Ascension, as narrated — Acts narrates appearances "during forty days" and that on the Mount of Olives "he was lifted up, and a cloud took him from their sight".[4]Acts gives forty days after Easter; Luke 24 appears to place the ascension at Bethany on Easter day itself.
35 ? Damascus, Syria The conversion of Paul — Acts narrates that on the road to Damascus a light flashed and a voice said, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?"[5] [25]A reckoning, not a record: Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia) holds it "not prior to 34" and proposes 35; the absolute date depends on the crucifixion year.
36 Jerusalem Pilate is recalled; Caiaphas is removed — Josephus writes that Pilate, "when he had tarried ten years in Judea, made haste to Rome", arriving after Tiberius had died; the Syrian legate Vitellius removes Caiaphas from the high priesthood.[11] [21] [22] [31]
40s
49 ? Rome, Italy Suetonius: Claudius expels Jews "at the instigation of Chrestus" — Suetonius writes that "since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus", Claudius expelled them from Rome — read by many as an early, garbled reference to Christ, by others as a different man.[13]Suetonius gives no year; 49 rests on the 5th-century Orosius, not read here. Whether "Chrestus" means Jesus is disputed.
60s
62 ? Jerusalem Josephus: James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", is stoned — Josephus narrates that the high priest Ananus convened the Sanhedrin and had James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", stoned.[11]The year is an inference from the interval between the procurators Festus and Albinus; Josephus gives no number.
64 Rome, Italy Tacitus: Nero blames the Christians for the fire of Rome — Tacitus records that Nero punished "Christians" for the Great Fire, and explains the name: "Christus, the founder of the name, had undergone the death penalty in the reign of Tiberius, by sentence of the procurator Pontius Pilatus".[12]Tacitus dates the fire by the consulship of Laecanius and Licinius, which is 64.
90s
93 ? Rome, Italy Josephus's Antiquities and the Testimonium Flavianum — The Antiquities contain a paragraph calling Jesus "a wise man, if it be lawful to call him a man" and saying "He was [the] Christ". Its authenticity has long been disputed; even the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1910 wrote that "the difficulty has not been definitively settled. The passage seems to suffer from repeated interpolations."[11] [24]93/94, the thirteenth year of Domitian — a date not confirmed in a source read here. The passage's authenticity is disputed.
110s
111 ? Bithynia, Turkey Pliny the Younger writes to Trajan about the Christians — Governing Bithynia, Pliny reports that Christians "met on a stated day before it was light, and addressed a form of prayer to Christ, as to a divinity" — a Roman official's description of early Christian worship.[14]Pliny's governorship of Bithynia is usually dated c. 111–113; no source read here gives the year.
130s
130 ? Jerusalem A temple is built over the site later venerated as the tomb — Eusebius writes that the tomb had been buried and covered with "a gloomy shrine of lifeless idols to the impure spirit whom they call Venus" — the site Constantine later cleared.[15] [36]Eusebius gives no year; the attribution to Hadrian's foundation of Aelia Capitolina, c. 130, is a tertiary source's.
320s
326 Jerusalem Constantine clears the site and the tomb is found — Eusebius narrates that when the ground was cleared, "contrary to all expectation, the venerable and hollowed monument of our Saviour's resurrection was discovered", and reproduces Constantine's letter ordering a church. The identification is Eusebius's and the Church's; the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that knowledge of the place was held to have been handed down by oral tradition. Eusebius does not mention the Cross here.[15] [28]c. 326, per the Catholic Encyclopedia; Eusebius gives no year.
327 ? Bethlehem / Jerusalem Helena founds churches at Bethlehem and on the Mount of Olives — Eusebius narrates that Constantine's mother Helena "dedicated two churches", one at the grotto of the Nativity and one "on the mount of his ascension".[15] [53] [33]The Catholic Encyclopedia dates the journey after 324; the Biblical Archaeology Society gives 327; Eusebius gives no year.
330s
335 Jerusalem The church of the Holy Sepulchre is dedicated — Bishops gather at Jerusalem for the dedication of Constantine's church at the tomb, per Eusebius.[16] [36] [28]13 September 335, per the tertiary source; the Catholic Encyclopedia gives 336. Eusebius places it in Constantine's thirtieth regnal year (July 335 – July 336).
339 ? Bethlehem The Church of the Nativity is consecrated — The basilica over the grotto venerated as the birthplace is consecrated, per the Biblical Archaeology Society, which adds that the church standing today is Justinian's 6th-century rebuilding.[33]31 May 339, per one popular-scholarly source; no institutional page confirming it was read.
340s
348 ? Jerusalem Cyril of Jerusalem: the wood of the Cross is already distributed — In his Catechetical Lectures, Bishop Cyril of Jerusalem says that "the whole world has since been filled with pieces of the wood of the Cross", taken from Jerusalem by the faithful — the earliest attestation of Cross relics this project located. Cyril does not name Helena.[71] [72]The Catechetical Lectures are dated c. 348–350.
380s
381 ? Jerusalem Egeria sees the wood and the title venerated on Good Friday — The pilgrim Egeria describes a silver-gilt casket brought out at Golgotha on Good Friday, from which "both the wood of the Cross and the title" are placed on a table and guarded by deacons, because someone had once bitten off a piece.[73]Egeria's pilgrimage is dated c. 381–384.
390s
395 Milan, Italy Ambrose credits Helena with finding the Cross — In his funeral oration for Theodosius, Ambrose of Milan narrates that Helena found three crosses and identified Christ's by its title, and that she found the nails — the first account this project located that connects Helena with the finding. Eusebius, writing earlier about her journey, does not; later writers such as Rufinus say the Cross was recognised by a miracle, not by the title.[74] [75] [73]Delivered on 25 February 395, per the Judaism and Rome commentary.
520s
525 ? Rome, Italy Dionysius Exiguus numbers the years from the Incarnation — Preparing an Easter table, the monk Dionysius Exiguus counts years "from the Incarnation" rather than from the persecuting emperor Diocletian — the origin of the Anno Domini era. By Matthew's account, which puts the birth before Herod's death in 4 BCE, the era's own epoch is several years late.[19] [18] [37]525 per the tertiary source; "about the year 527" per the Catholic Encyclopedia.
610s
614 ? Jerusalem The Persians take the relic of the Cross from Jerusalem — Persian forces capture Jerusalem and carry off the relic of the Cross, per the Custody of the Holy Land and the Catholic Encyclopedia.[77] [76] [92]The Custody of the Holy Land and the Catholic Encyclopedia's "Jerusalem" give 614; the same encyclopedia's "Holy Lance" gives 615.
630s
630 ? Jerusalem Heraclius restores the Cross to Jerusalem — The Byzantine emperor Heraclius brings the relic back to Jerusalem. The Catholic Encyclopedia links the feast of the Exaltation of the Cross (14 September) to his return — a feast, an act of cult.[77] [76]The Custody of the Holy Land gives 630; the Catholic Encyclopedia gives 629.
1070s
1075 1075-03-13 ? Oviedo, Spain The Arca Santa of Oviedo is opened: the Sudarium enters the record — At the solemn opening of the Arca Santa before King Alfonso VI, the relics listed include "de sudario Domini", per the cathedral — the first unambiguous attestation of the cloth now called the Sudarium of Oviedo. The cathedral's account of the ark's journey from Jerusalem in 614 is its tradition.[57]13 March 1075 per Oviedo Cathedral; secondary accounts give 14 March. The record of the opening itself was not read.
1090s
1098 ? Antioch (Antakya), Turkey A lance is "found" at Antioch — During the First Crusade, the Provençal pilgrim Peter Bartholomew reports visions of the apostle Andrew and leads a dig that produces a lance, per the chronicler Raymond d'Aguiliers. The Catholic Encyclopedia calls it a "discovery under very questionable circumstances"; which, if any, of the surviving lances it was is disputed.[96] [92]June 1098, during the siege of Antioch; the day was not established.
1100s
1101 Genoa, Italy The Sacro Catino reaches Genoa — Guglielmo Embriaco brings to Genoa a green hexagonal dish long held to be emerald and later called the Holy Grail, per the Italian Ministry of Culture. In 1806 it was established to be glass.[106]
1150s
1156 Argenteuil, France The Argenteuil tunic is first attested — A charter records an exposition of the tunic at Argenteuil organised by the Archbishop of Rouen in the presence of Louis VII — the first attestation, per the custodian parish, whose tradition dates the tunic's arrival to Charlemagne's time.[66]
1180s
1187 1187-07-04 Horns of Hattin, Israel The crusaders' relic of the Cross is lost at Hattin — At the battle of Hattin the crusader army carries its relic of the Cross and loses it: "The Holy Cross also was lost," one contemporary account records; another reports a Templar's claim to have buried it, and a search that found nothing.[78]
1190s
1196 1196-05-01 Trier, Germany The Holy Tunic of Trier is first attested — Archbishop Johann I encloses the tunic in the new high altar of Trier Cathedral, per the Gesta Treverorum as the diocese reports it. The diocese itself describes the story of Helena sending the tunic to Trier as a legend: "a historical event was invented to explain a state of affairs".[63] [64]
1230s
1239 1239-08-19 Paris, France Louis IX brings the Crown of Thorns to Paris — Having redeemed the Crown from the Venetian bankers to whom the Latin emperors of Constantinople had pledged it, Louis IX receives it at Villeneuve-l'Archevêque on 10 August and carries it barefoot into Paris on 19 August, per the Diocese of Paris.[87]
1240s
1248 1248-04-26 Paris, France The Sainte-Chapelle is consecrated to house the Crown — The royal chapel Louis IX built for the Crown and his other Passion relics is consecrated. The Crown stays there until the Revolution; the Sainte-Chapelle does not hold it today.[89] [88] [87]
1290s
1291 Bruges, Belgium The Holy Blood procession is first mentioned — A by-law of the Bruges porters' guild contains the oldest mention of the procession, per the Basilica of the Holy Blood — which also records that most historians strongly doubt the story of the relic's arrival in 1150 and trace the oldest reliable sources to the 13th century.[69]
1350s
1353 ? Lirey, France The Shroud appears at Lirey — The knight Geoffroi de Charny founds a collegiate church at Lirey in which a cloth bearing the double image of a man is shown for veneration — the first unambiguous appearance of the object now kept in Turin.[55] [54]The Catholic Encyclopedia's "Troyes" dates the founding of the Lirey church to 20 June 1353; its "Holy Shroud" says "we first hear of it about the year 1360". The documentary start of the cloth is dated c. 1353–1360.
1380s
1389 ? Troyes, France Bishop Pierre d'Arcis: the Lirey cloth is "cunningly painted" — In a memorandum to the Avignon pope Clement VII, the Bishop of Troyes writes that an earlier inquiry by his predecessor had found the cloth "cunningly painted, the truth being attested by the artist who had painted it". The memorandum is his account; defenders of the Shroud dispute its status.[56] [54]c. 1389. The memorandum survives as an unsigned, undated draft; whether it was sent is disputed.
1390s
1390 1390-01-06 ? Avignon, France Clement VII rules on the Lirey expositions — read two ways — The Avignon pope issues bulls on the Lirey cloth. The Catholic Encyclopedia's "Troyes" says they "approved the exposition as lawful"; its "Holy Shroud" says the pope required the priest to "declare in a loud voice that it was not the real shroud of Christ, but only a picture made to represent it". Either way the act regulates exposition; it is not a ruling that the cloth is authentic.[55] [54]The bulls themselves were not read; both readings below are the Catholic Encyclopedia's, in two different articles.
1490s
1492 Vatican City Sultan Bayezid II sends a lance to Innocent VIII — A lance kept in Constantinople is given to Pope Innocent VIII by the Ottoman sultan, per Vatican News; it is still exposed in St Peter's in Lent. The Catholic Encyclopedia records that "great doubts as to its authenticity were felt at Rome" at the time, because of rival lances.[93] [92]
1492 1492-02-01 Rome, Italy The Titulus Crucis is found at Santa Croce — During works at Santa Croce in Gerusalemme, a lead box is found bearing the inscription "HIC EST TITULUS VERAE CRUCIS", per the diarist Stefano Infessura; three other contemporaries give differing versions of where and how. A Leiden medievalist reads the timing as tied to the fall of Granada; the basilica calls the tablet a relic "probably" brought there in the 6th century.[84] [82]
1530s
1532 Chambéry, France The Shroud is damaged by fire at Chambéry — A fire in the Savoy chapel at Chambéry leaves the burn lines and holes still visible on the cloth, per the custodian archdiocese.[39]The year is the custodian's; the day was not verified.
1540s
1543 Geneva, Switzerland Calvin's Treatise on Relics — Jean Calvin argues that the pieces of the Cross shown across Europe would fill "a good big ship" though the Gospel says one man could carry it — the classic Protestant case against the relics, answered in 1870 by Rohault de Fleury.[79]
1570s
1576 Monza, Italy Borromeo establishes the cult of the Holy Nail at Monza — Archbishop Carlo Borromeo establishes the cult of the Sacred Nail "to make official the recognition of the diadem as a relic", per the Monza cathedral museum — an act of cult about the Iron Crown's inner band. The same museum reports recent studies dating the crown to between the 4th–5th and the 9th centuries.[86]
1578 1578-09-14 Turin, Italy The Shroud is moved to Turin — The Dukes of Savoy move the cloth from Chambéry to Turin — to shorten the pilgrimage of Archbishop Carlo Borromeo of Milan, per the custodian archdiocese.[41]
1580s
1589 Rome, Italy Sixtus V moves the Scala Sancta — The stairs venerated as those of Pilate's palace are moved to their present site beside the Lateran. The tradition that Helena brought them to Rome is attributed by the Catholic Encyclopedia to "the historians of the monument".[100] [98]
1800s
1806 1806-08-10 Paris, France The Crown of Thorns is assigned to Notre-Dame — After the Revolution moved it to Saint-Denis and the national library, the Crown is handed to the Archbishop of Paris and placed in the treasury of Notre-Dame, per the Diocese of Paris.[87]
1850s
1853 1853-06-13 Rome, Italy Pius IX entrusts the Scala Sancta to the Passionists — By the constitution Decet Romanum Pontificem, and a second act of 1854, Pius IX gives the sanctuary's custody to the Passionist Fathers, per the sanctuary — acts about custody, not about the stairs' origin.[99]
1870s
1870 Paris, France Rohault de Fleury measures the relics of the Cross — Answering Calvin, the antiquarian Charles Rohault de Fleury estimates the known Cross relics at about 5 million cubic millimetres and argues that, even tripled for relics unknown, they make under a tenth of the 180 million he computes for a cross. The Catholic Encyclopedia later restates his figures slightly differently; neither calculation is established fact.[80] [81]
1890s
1898 1898-05-25 Turin, Italy Secondo Pia photographs the Shroud — During an exposition, the lawyer Secondo Pia, authorised by the House of Savoy, photographs the cloth for the first time; his negative shows a positive-looking image, per the custodian archdiocese.[42]The exposition ran from 25 May to 2 June 1898; the photograph was taken during it.
1960s
1961 Caesarea Maritima, Israel The Pilate inscription is found at Caesarea — An Italian excavation finds a limestone dedication naming "[Pon]tius Pilatus … [Praef]ectus Iudae[ae]", reused in the theatre; it is now in the Israel Museum.[35] [32]
1970s
1978 Turin, Italy STURP examines the Shroud — After the fourth-centenary exposition, 44 scholars — among them the American Shroud of Turin Research Project — examine the cloth for 120 hours, per the custodian. STURP's 1981 summary reports no pigments on the fibrils and a real human form; the microscopist Walter McCrone argued the image was paint. The disagreement is recorded, not resolved.[43] [51] [52]
1980s
1983 1983-03-18 Turin, Italy The Shroud passes to the Holy See — At the death of Umberto II of Savoy, the cloth passes by his bequest to the Holy See, which has owned it since; the Archbishop of Turin keeps it as Pontifical Custodian.[38] [40]
1989 1989-02-16 Tucson, Arizona, USA / Oxford, England / Zurich, Switzerland Radiocarbon dating places the Shroud's linen in 1260–1390 — The Arizona, Oxford and Zurich laboratories report in Nature that "the age of the shroud is obtained as AD 1260-1390, with at least 95% confidence". Later critiques contest the sample and the statistics, among them a 2019 analysis of the raw data in Archaeometry; the result and the critiques are each their authors'.[48] [49] [50]
1990s
1990 Jerusalem The Caiaphas ossuary is found — Construction work south of the Old City exposes a family tomb; one ossuary is inscribed "Yehosef bar Qayafa", Joseph son of Caiaphas. Its identification with the Gospels' high priest is held possible by some scholars and challenged by others.[31] [32]
1997 1997-04-11 Turin, Italy Fire in Turin Cathedral; the Shroud is carried out undamaged — Fire destroys Guarini's chapel; the cloth, moved to the choir in 1993, is carried to safety and found to have suffered no damage, per the custodian.[44]
1998 1998-05-24 Turin, Italy John Paul II: the Church "has no specific competence to pronounce" — Visiting the exposition, John Paul II says: "Since it is not a matter of faith, the Church has no specific competence to pronounce on these questions. She entrusts to scientists the task of continuing to investigate". He calls the cloth "a mirror of the Gospel" — a devotional reading, not a verdict either way.[46]
2000s
2002 Rome, Italy Radiocarbon dating places the Titulus in 980–1146 — Physicists of Roma Tre University report in Radiocarbon that the walnut tablet dates to AD 980–1146 (two sigma), and note that this "permits one to consider" that it is a copy of an original. The result is the laboratory's.[83]
2004 Argenteuil, France The Argenteuil tunic is radiocarbon-dated to 530–650 — A dating by the French CEA laboratory at Saclay gives "between the years 530 and 650", per the custodian parish, which reports the researchers' warning about contamination and maintains its case for authenticity. A 2005 test on another fragment, commissioned privately, gave 670–880 and is contested.[66] [68]
2004 Vienna, Austria Vienna's Holy Lance is dated to the 8th century — A University of Vienna study, with the gold and silver sleeves removed for the first time since 1924, finds a winged lance of a form "typical of the second half of the 8th century". The Kunsthistorisches Museum, which keeps it, writes that its central pin later came to be venerated as a Nail and the lance, in the 13th century, as that of Longinus.[95] [94]The sleeves were removed in February 2004 for the study of 2003–2004.
2006 2006-09-01 Manoppello, Italy Benedict XVI visits the Volto Santo of Manoppello — The pope makes what the text calls a private pilgrimage to the Capuchin shrine, speaking of the Holy Face; the address does not use the words "veil" or "relic". The shrine identifies its image with the Veronica, which St Peter's still exposes as its own.[61] [62]

Parallel storylines

The chronology read as parallel storylines: one row per lane, one column per decade, each cell the number of that lane’s events in that decade. An event that belongs to several storylines is counted in each, so the rows sum to more than the number of events. Select a number to jump to that decade in the chronology.

These lanes are an editorial reading of this chronology, not a neutral index. They separate what the Gospels narrate and scholars date, what non-Christian writers and the ground itself attest, and the history of the objects venerated as relics — so that a reader can see which kind of evidence stands behind each entry. They are not a ranking of that evidence: the lane an event sits in says what kind of source it rests on, not whether it is true. Left out on purpose: the history of doctrine and of the Church, which belong to other chronologies.

What each lane is grounded in
  1. The life, as the sources date it — The events of Jesus's life as the Gospels narrate them, placed in time by the anchors the texts themselves give (Herod, the census of Quirinius, the fifteenth year of Tiberius, Pontius Pilate) and by the date ranges of modern scholarship, each attributed.
  2. Outside witnesses and memory-sites — What non-Christian ancient writers (Josephus, Tacitus, Pliny, Suetonius) and archaeology say, and the first churches built on the places the Gospels name — each cited to the text, the object or its custodian.
  3. The objects venerated as relics — Each object's first attestation, custody, transfers, tests and the acts of Church authorities about it, one act about one object at a time (core ADR-0007, applied to relics).

Disambiguation & nuance

This subject is contested terrain twice over: between faith and historical criticism about the life, and between tradition and the laboratory about the relics. These distinctions are the ones readers most often flatten.

The Jesus of history ≠ the Christ of faith

Two registers, both recorded here and neither allowed to settle the other: what historical method reconstructs from the sources — that Jesus was executed under Pilate, attested by the Gospels and Tacitus — and what the Church professes, that he is the Christ, the Son of God, risen. The site writes "the Gospels narrate" and "Christians profess" for the second, and names the historian for the first.[12] [6]

BCE / CE, and the error in Anno Domini

BCE and CE number the years exactly as BC and AD do. Dionysius Exiguus counted from the Incarnation, but by Matthew's account the birth fell before Herod's death in 4 BCE — so, by the era's own numbering, "before Christ". There is no year zero: 1 BCE is followed by 1 CE.[18] [19]

Julian dates

Every ancient date here — 7 April 30, 3 April 33, 13 September 335 — is in the Julian calendar used at the time, not converted to the Gregorian.[29]

Prefect ≠ procurator

The Caesarea inscription gives Pilate's title as praefectus; Tacitus and later writers call him procurator, the title the office bore later. Both appear in the sources quoted here.[35] [12]

Three Herods

Herod the Great, in whose reign Matthew sets the birth, died in 4 BCE; Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee, appears in the ministry and the Passion; Herod Agrippa I appears in Acts. They are different men.[20] [2]

The day of the crucifixion: 14 or 15 Nisan

In the Synoptic Gospels the Last Supper is a Passover meal and Jesus dies the next day; in John he dies on the day of preparation, before the Passover. Both place the death on a Friday. Harmonizations exist and are one view among several.[7] [10] [29]

Shroud ≠ Sudarium

The Shroud of Turin is a full-length linen bearing the double image of a man; the Sudarium of Oviedo is a face cloth with stains and no image. They are different objects in different cities; the claim that they fit together is that of the Spanish sindonologists, not a finding.[39] [58]

Veronica ≠ Manoppello ≠ Mandylion

Three objects, three legends: the Veronica is the veil of the Way of the Cross kept at St Peter's; the Volto Santo of Manoppello is an image its Capuchin shrine identifies with the lost original, which St Peter's does not concede; the Mandylion is the image the Abgar legend ties to Edessa.[62] [60]

Titulus ≠ True Cross

The Titulus is a separate object with a separate history: first recorded in Rome in 1492 and radiocarbon-dated to 980–1146. No dating of the Cross wood was located.[83] [82]

Several Holy Lances

The lances of St Peter's, Vienna, Etchmiadzin and Kraków are separate objects with separate histories; the lance "found" at Antioch in 1098 is identified with more than one of them by different authors.[92] [94]

An act of cult is not a verdict of authenticity

Expositions, feasts, indulgences, jubilees, basilicas and papal visits honour or regulate veneration; they are not rulings that an object is what tradition says. The Holy See's 2017 instruction on relics concerns relics of the Blessed and the Saints. This site records each act about each object as what it is.[109] [46]

A laboratory date is its laboratory's

Radiocarbon and other test results are recorded with their authors and journals, and published critiques as critiques. A date range dates the sample tested, not the tradition attached to the object.[48] [50]
